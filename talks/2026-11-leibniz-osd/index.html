Synthetic participants

  • Large language models have rapidly become a standard research instrument in the social and behavioural sciences.
  • A growing body of work uses them as “synthetic participants” to generate survey responses and experimental decisions (Argyle et al., 2023; Dillion et al., 2023; Cui et al., 2025),
  • to replicate stylised facts of human behaviour in economic games (Polachek et al., 2025; Akata et al., 2025; Pal et al., 2026),
  • and to forecast how human samples will respond to a treatment before the human study is run (Hewitt et al., 2026).
  • The appeal is straightforward: synthetic samples are fast, cheap and replenishable, which is particularly attractive for pre-testing policy interventions that are expensive, slow or politically costly to field.
[0:50] Set the scene. Three uses: generate responses, replicate stylised facts, forecast human results. Then the appeal - fast, cheap, replenishable. Do not dwell on the citations; the point is that this is now normal practice. THE PAPERS, one line each, if anyone asks. - Argyle et al. (2023), Political Analysis: "Out of one, many" - conditioning GPT-3 on demographic backstories reproduces US survey response distributions; the paper that made "silicon sampling" a method. - Dillion et al. (2023), TiCS: can AI language models replace human participants? Reports a very high correlation between GPT moral judgements and human ratings, and still argues against replacement. - Cui et al. (2025), Nature Computational Science: large-scale LLM replication of scenario-based experiments in psychology and management. - Polachek et al. (2025), SSRN: ultimatum-game responders, human vs LLM. - Akata et al. (2025), Nature Human Behaviour: LLMs in repeated games - they cooperate in some, fail at coordination in others. - Pal et al. (2026), PNAS Nexus: LLMs instantiate evolutionarily robust cooperation strategies. - Hewitt et al. (2026), Nature: LLMs predict the results of social science experiments - the forecasting use case, and the strongest claim of the set.

Model dependence

  • LLM responses to identical prompts vary substantially across providers, model versions and time (Bisbee et al., 2024; Sclar et al., 2024).
  • Some authors have responded by running a handful of models and checking whether the headline finding survives.
  • What is missing is a quantitative measure of how much of the variation in effect sizes is attributable to the choice of the model.
[1:00] The gap. Emphasise the word "effect sizes" - not response distributions, not accuracy: the estimand researchers actually report. Then the implication, which the closing frame states in full: when synthetic pre-tests inform whether interventions are fielded, scaled or abandoned, substantive conclusions may become partly contingent on the provider or model selected. - Bisbee et al. (2024), Political Analysis: "Synthetic replacements for human survey data? The perils of LLMs" - ANES replication; GPT gets the means roughly right but the variance badly wrong, and answers move with the model version. - Sclar et al. (2024): formatting sensitivity - separators, spacing and ordering alone move performance substantially on an otherwise identical task.

Population heterogeneity

  • Holzmeister et al. (2024) decompose heterogeneity in effect-size estimates into population, design and analytical sources, each isolated by holding the other two fixed.
  • Following this framework, we argue that LLM-induced heterogeneity can be understood as a form of population heterogeneity: the design (one prompt per condition) and the analysis (one estimation path) are held fixed.
  • Each model constitutes a potential synthetic population from which researchers can sample; as in many-labs studies, each LLM serves as an independently collected study population.
Hypothesis 1 (pre-registered): There is significant between-LLM heterogeneity (τ > 0) in effect sizes.

Candidate variables that may explain this heterogeneity, such as the model’s provider, are assessed in clearly labeled exploratory analyses.

[1:05] The framing that makes the project a measurement exercise. Stress the isolation logic: design and analysis held fixed by construction, so tau can directly be interpreted as population heterogeneity. Then H1 - and be explicit that everything about providers is exploratory.

Design

[1:00] The design in one picture: Case 1 on arrival, Case 2 on the click. Case 1 is a conceptual replication with a human benchmark; Case 2 is a direct replication we extend with the trust game. Two treatments x three games plus one treatment x two outcomes = eight cells, each of which becomes one random-effects meta-analysis over the panel.

Case 1: Nudging intervention for Carbon Pricing Support

[0:55] The concrete manipulation. First the part both arms share, in one breath: a welcome and consent block - project description, compensation, confidentiality - then general information saying the study has one survey question and one incentivized decision task, and then the climate introduction, "A Warming Planet", on the costs of greenhouse-gas emissions. That is the whole common stem. Then point at the three orange blocks: that is the entire treatment. Two outcomes per respondent - stated support on a five-point scale, revealed support as a donation out of a potential 200-euro lottery win. 200 observations per arm, 400 per model. The human benchmark is a representative Austrian sample of n = 1,600, and both prompts reproduce the welcome information, the baseline climate text, the tax information text, the stated-support question and the incentivized donation task of Schwaiger et al. (2026).

Case 2: First-Mover actions in Classic Economic Games

  • Dictator, ultimatum and trust games. The DG and UG prompts are verbatim from Polachek et al. (2026); the trustor prompt is our extension.
  • Two binary treatments crossed orthogonally:
    • Wealth — stated initial endowment of 493 vs. 0 Rupees;
    • Human emphasis — an added “Respond as if you are human.” instruction.
  • Five-level stake ladder (20 to 200,000 Rupees) as a covariate ⇒ 20 cells × 100 observations = 2,000 per LLM and game.

Estimation pipeline: one model

[1:50] THE CORE FRAME - take your time; the whole pipeline is on screen at once. (1) one model; (2) the model is a potential synthetic population, and the N = 400 responses are a sample from it; (3) half get control, half get treatment - randomisation is by construction, we simply send 200 of each; (4) the two prompts, differing only in the three tax blocks; (5) every query returns both outcomes - stated support on 1 to 5, and the donation out of the 200-euro lottery win; (6) the two response distributions and the shift between their means; (7) one OLS per outcome, with HC3 robust standard errors; (8) divide by the pooled SD - Huber et al. (2023) - and you have Cohen's d with its SE. These are the real responses of gpt-5-2025-08-07: the information treatment LOWERS its stated support, d = -0.46. In the forest plot of the results you will find it as one of the rows near the top. The donation runs through the identical path (d = -0.48 for this model). Say clearly: nothing here is a chat session - stateless, single-turn API calls at provider defaults, no system prompt, no conversation history, no tools.

Meta-analysis summary

[1:00] The deliverable of the confirmatory analysis: one column per cell, one tau each, full panel. Do not read it out. Point at the tau row and the p row. The study-level criterion: H1 is supported within a case if Q rejects at p < 0.005 in both cells of Case 1 and in at least four of six cells of Case 2 - it rejects in all eight. Robustness, if asked: the 47 registered models only - all eight rejected, tau 0.21 to 0.53; the balanced panel of 32 registered models with a defined d in every cell - tau 0.19 to 0.40; pooling the unstandardised OLS coefficients on the raw scale - all eight rejected. The lower bound of the 99.5% CI for tau exceeds 0.06 in every cell, and the 95% prediction interval includes zero in every cell.

Conclusion

  • We quantify how much the effect sizes of synthetic pre-tests depend on which model is queried: each of 63 LLMs from ten providers serves as an independently collected study population, and we pool the per-LLM effects of two cases (eight treatment–outcome cells) in random-effects meta-analyses.
  • Homogeneity is rejected in every cell. The estimates of τ range from 0.21 to 0.50, with a median of 0.33, which is about 5.5 times the median between-population heterogeneity of τ ≈ 0.06 reported by Holzmeister et al. (2024).
  • An effect estimated from a single LLM should not be interpreted as an estimate of “the” synthetic effect: the uncertainty attributable to model choice is often larger than the sampling uncertainty within a model.
  • Synthetic pre-tests should be based on a broad panel of models, with the between-model variation reported alongside the pooled effect.
[1:00] Close on the findings, then the implications. The median tau is about five times the median heterogeneity Holzmeister et al. report across human populations: if each LLM is one synthetic population, these populations differ from one another more than human populations do. That is the missing error bar of single-model studies. Our tau estimates can serve as a reference for uncertainty assessments and power calculations, e.g. reported as a prediction interval. LIMITATIONS, if asked: the panel is a snapshot of a rapidly changing market; each model was queried at provider defaults, single-turn, with a single prompt per condition - so prompt wording, decoding parameters or persona conditioning would likely add to the uncertainty we document; and the Case 2 prompts and results are published, so they may be in the training data (this does not affect the estimand). ANTICIPATED QUESTIONS. - "Your null is a straw man - nobody thinks tau is exactly zero." Agree: the interesting quantity is the magnitude of tau and the width of the prediction interval, not the rejection. The design has no same-model-twice control, so within-model run-to-run instability is not separated from between-model variation. - "Is it just response bias?" A directional bias that shifts a model's answers uniformly in both conditions would cancel out in a treatment contrast (cf. Meyer et al. 2026) - yet we still find large between-model differences in treatment effects. - "What are the API settings?" Provider defaults throughout, one stateless single-turn call per observation, no system prompt, no tools. - "Which model is closest to humans?" We deliberately make no such claim; the human benchmark is depicted, the comparisons are exploratory. - "What next?" Varying prompts and models in a crossed design, to weigh model choice against prompt choice; and model characteristics such as size, release date or post-training that predict the response.
